● The actions are probably surface-level for three reasons: the generator gets
  little specific information, the prompt asks for little, and the scoring
  filters out bold ideas. Here are ideas for each, ordered by how much I'd
  expect them to help.

  1. The scoring currently punishes challenging actions

  This is probably the biggest hidden cause. All three per-consideration
  measures score 1 as favourable:

  - volatility: 1 = the consideration is barely disturbed
  - risk: 1 = no risk
  - valence: 1 = strong benefit

  Two of the three reward actions that change nothing and risk nothing. So "make
  a bullet-point list" scores near-perfect on volatility and risk. A bold
  action loses those points, and the pruning step then deletes the bottom 5 of
  every batch, which is probably where bold actions end up. Even if Opus
  generates challenging ideas, they likely get filtered out before you see them.

  Options:
  - Risk appetite slider. One setting that runs from "prefer safe" to "prefer
    bold" and flips how volatility and risk count.
  - Score upside separately from downside. For example, "how much could this
    change your situation for the better" next to "how bad is the worst case". A
    bold action can then win on upside even with a real downside.
  - Exempt high-surprisingness actions from pruning, so variety survives the
    queue.

  2. The generator only sees highly compressed, general information

  Opus currently gets just the considerations: at most 7 + 21 lines of 10 words
  or less, written to be "fairly general". All the specifics you actually wrote
  are lost: names, places, fields, skills, numbers, projects, people you know.
  Generic input gives generic output.

  Options:
  - Feed the raw data too. Your prompt answers and all comments are small; Opus
    can easily read them in full. The considerations would then act as a lens,
    not the only input.
  - Feed the history. The actions you've already seen and your opinion on each.
    The generator can then avoid repeats, build on what you liked, and see why
    you rejected things.
  - Ask for more specific facts. Add prompts or follow-up questions for concrete
    details: field, city, skills with levels, money and time available, people
    in your network, things you've already tried.

  3. The prompt asks for little

  "Two sentences: what to do, and why it fits" invites short, generic advice.

  Options:
  - Require concreteness. Each action names something specific (an organisation,
    a type of person, a place, a program), the first step you could take this
    week, rough time and cost, and what doing it would teach you or prove.
  - Require a real stake. Count something as an action only if it involves
    commitment: money, a public commitment, the chance of being rejected, a
    deadline, or putting your own work out there. Ban pure preparation steps
    such as making a list or asking for advice, unless they're part of something
    bigger.
  - Spread actions across levels of commitment. For example, some small
    experiments for this week, some one-month commitments, and some 6–12-month
    bets, like the current obvious/unconventional split.

  4. Use better ideation techniques

  - Challenge the constraints. Pick one consideration or "don't want", assume it
    no longer applies, and ask what becomes possible. Then check whether the
    constraint was real.
  - Combine considerations. Ask for actions that serve two unrelated
    considerations at once in an unusual way; these tend to be the most personal
    ideas.
  - Use people like you as examples. "What have people with a similar background
    actually done?" grounds ideas in real paths.
  - Ask for the bold version. For each idea, also generate a much more ambitious
    version and let the scoring choose.

  5. Give the best ideas a second step

  Your planned phase 3 already covers developing promising actions. A lighter
  version could fit now:
  - When an action reaches the top of the queue, or you comment positively on
    it, a second Opus call turns it into a concrete mini-plan: steps, the first
    move, what could go wrong, and how you'd know it's working.
  - Web search could add real opportunities: specific programs, open roles,
    grants, events with deadlines. That's what makes ideas concrete, but it
    costs more per call and adds latency.

     get pruned.
  2. Feed the raw data and the shown-action history to the generator 
     (section 2). It's cheap and probably the biggest jump in specificity.
  3. Tighten the prompt (section 3) with concrete fields and a required stake.
